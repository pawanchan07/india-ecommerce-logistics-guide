The industry in one screen

Read this and you will understand roughly 70% of what matters. Everything after it is mechanism, evidence and edge cases.

An Indian consumer buys a ₹1,500 product online. The merchant does not deliver it. Instead the merchant buys a slot in somebody else's network — usually for ₹60–₹110 — and that network moves the parcel through a chain of hubs it mostly leases, on trucks it mostly does not own, into the hands of a delivery executive who is mostly not its employee. The consumer pays ₹1,500, of which the logistics industry sees only the shipping fee. The merchant's real logistics cost, however, is not the shipping fee; it is the shipping fee plus the cost of every parcel that comes back, and in cash-on-delivery India roughly one in four of those parcels does come back.

So the whole industry is a machine for converting drop density — parcels per square kilometre per hour — into margin, while pushing as much of the volatility as possible onto people who are paid per parcel. Everything else follows from that sentence.

The five relationships that explain everything

Each arrow below is a separate commercial negotiation with its own price, its own risk transfer and its own information asymmetry. Almost every strategic question in this industry is really a question about one of these five arrows.

Ten things worth knowing before you go deeper

  1. The parcel is not the product. Density is.A carrier's cost per parcel is set almost entirely by how many drops it can make per square kilometre per hour. Two carriers with identical trucks and identical wages will have completely different unit costs if one has twice the drop density. This is why scale in logistics is not a vanity metric — it is the metric. → §05
  2. Roughly three-quarters of a big carrier's revenue immediately leaves again.Delhivery's "freight, handling and servicing cost" was 73.2% of revenue in FY25; its own payroll was 15.4%. The network is largely rented — third-party trucks, contracted manpower, partner delivery centres. That is a deliberate choice, not an accident. → §04
  3. Most people who touch your parcel don't work for the company on the label.Delhivery reported 24,300+ on-roll employees against 37,000+ off-roll and 44,700+ gig workers (FY25). Ecom Express disclosed that 71.42% of its FY24 deliveries were completed by gig workers. → §09
  4. Yields fall every single year — on purpose.Delhivery's revenue per express parcel fell from ₹70.7 (FY25) to ₹63.4 (FY26). Average order value is falling too. Carriers convert scale into lower prices to buy volume, because volume buys density, and density is the only durable advantage. → §03
  5. Productivity gains are split — and the platform sets the split.Ecom Express states the mechanism openly: as density rises, "we are able to reduce our per-shipment payout while the delivery executive still benefits from an overall increase in earnings". Both statements are true simultaneously. That sentence is the labour economics of the entire sector. → §07
  6. Cash on delivery is the industry's hidden tax.COD orders return at roughly 26%; prepaid at under 2%. Every returned parcel burns two freight legs and earns the merchant nothing. COD is why Indian e-commerce logistics is structurally less profitable than its Chinese or American equivalent. → §06
  7. Speed is a returns instrument, not a luxury.Shipments delivered in 1–2 days return at 22%; those taking 5+ days return at 35%. A cheap slow carrier manufactures its own failures — which is why the lowest shipping rate is frequently the most expensive option a merchant can choose. → §11
  8. Aggregators sell relief from fragmentation, not transport.Shiprocket passes roughly 74% of merchant payments straight through to 42 courier partners and keeps ~18% as contribution margin. It is a software and risk-management business wearing a logistics coat. → §12
  9. Fixed cost sits in concrete and code; variable cost sits in people.Sort centres, automation and technology are fixed. Linehaul, last-mile payouts and contract manpower are variable. Operating leverage only appears when volume grows into capacity you already built — which is exactly what Delhivery's FY26 numbers show. → §04
  10. The binding constraint is shifting from capital to labour and regulation.India's four Labour Codes came into force on 21 November 2025, and Karnataka's gig-worker welfare fee went live on 13 February 2026. For the first time, the cost of the workforce model is being set partly outside the company. → §10

Five business models that look identical from the outside

These companies are all called "logistics companies" and they compete for the same parcels, but they have different balance sheets, different failure modes and different reasons to exist.

A · Traditional express courier — Blue Dart, DTDC

Revenue model. Premium price per shipment for a guaranteed, usually time-definite, service. Blue Dart's revenue per shipment in FY24 was approximately ₹147 — more than double Delhivery's express parcel and nearly three times Ecom Express's. That gap is the whole strategy.

Cost structure. Heavier fixed base: Blue Dart uniquely operates its own aircraft fleet through Blue Dart Aviation. Air capacity is expensive and can't be flexed down in a weak quarter, but it is also the reason it can promise next-morning delivery on lanes where surface carriers need three days.

Workforce. The most employee-heavy model in the sector, and the most exposed to the new Labour Codes — which Blue Dart explicitly cited as raising employee-benefit and freight/handling costs in FY26.

Who buys it. Documents, pharmaceuticals, high-value electronics, B2B where the recipient is a business that will definitely be present, and any shipment where a failed delivery costs far more than the freight.

Weakness. Structurally unable to win price-led e-commerce volume. FY26 revenue grew 7.4% to ₹6,141 Cr while Delhivery's service revenue grew ~17%. It is not losing — it is deliberately not playing.

DTDC's variant substitutes franchise density for aviation: 14,000+ franchise outlets convert fixed retail cost into somebody else's problem, and buy small-town reach cheaply. Company claim

B · Technology-enabled parcel network — Delhivery, Ecom Express, XpressBees

Revenue model. Low price per parcel, enormous volume, engineered cost. Delhivery moved 1,054 million express parcels in FY26 at ₹63.4 each. The business is won on cost per shipment, and cost per shipment is won on density and automation.

Cost structure. Fixed investment in sortation (Delhivery: 45 automated sort centres, 8.2 million parcels/day capacity) sitting on top of a heavily variabilised movement layer. Freight, handling and servicing cost — payments to third parties — was 73.2% of revenue in FY25.

Workforce. A deliberately hybrid stack: a thin on-roll core, a large off-roll contracted layer and a very large gig layer that flexes with festive peaks.

Why it wins. It is the only model whose unit cost falls structurally as it grows. Delhivery's transport ROIC went from 5.2% (FY25) to 16.0% (FY26) without a proportionate rise in invested capital — that is operating leverage arriving.

Why it's brutal. The same mechanism forces prices down. XpressBees shows the failure case: FY25 revenue was flat at ₹2,874 Cr while losses widened 85% to ₹370 Cr, with large clients renegotiating rates. Sub-scale players in this model get squeezed from both ends.

Consolidation is the endgame. Delhivery acquired Ecom Express in 2025 — the clearest possible signal that this model has room for very few winners.

Asset ownershipMedium — owns sortation, rents movement.
WorkforceHybrid; gig share rising deliberately.
Pricing powerLow and falling — that is the strategy, not a failure.
ScalabilityVery high, above a density threshold.
Fails whenVolume stalls before fixed cost is absorbed.
C · Logistics aggregator — Shiprocket

Owns no trucks and no hubs. Buys carrier capacity in bulk, resells it in single units to ~215,000 merchants, and layers software on top. 73.8% of its FY26 revenue went straight back out as "cost of merchant solutions"; it retained 18.34% as contribution margin and 0.87% as adjusted EBITDA. Its core shipping business is profitable at 12.56% adjusted EBITDA margin; its emerging businesses consumed nearly all of that. Full analysis in §12.

D · Gig / crowdsourced last mile — Shadowfax

Treats delivery capacity as a spot market. Shadowfax handled 436 million orders in FY25 on ₹2,485 Cr of revenue — about ₹57 per order — with a ~2% adjusted EBITDA margin and its first full-year profit. It reports 205,000+ active delivery partners against roughly 3 million verified riders, which tells you the model's real nature: an enormous, mostly dormant pool activated on demand.

The structural advantage is that capacity costs nothing when idle. The structural weakness is that quality is hard to guarantee when the person delivering has no relationship with anyone, and its own IPO filing warns that reliance on non-exclusive gig partners could disrupt operations.

E · Captive marketplace logistics — Ekart, Amazon Transportation Services

Built to serve one marketplace, optimised for its own demand curve, and historically closed to outsiders. Both have now opened: Ekart opened its network to third-party MSMEs and D2C brands with 300+ franchise outlets and a target of 1,000 by end-2026; Amazon opened Amazon Supply Chain Services beyond its own marketplace.

Redseer's data explains why captives cannot simply take the whole market: captive arms reach roughly 12,000 pin codes against 3PLs' ~27,000. Captives are dense where their marketplace is dense and absent everywhere else — precisely the opposite of what a growing tier-2+ market needs.

The taxonomy is leaky, and that matters

Delhivery runs a technology network and a franchise-like partner delivery-centre layer and a fulfilment business and a truckload marketplace. Shiprocket runs an aggregator and owns warehouses and lends money to merchants. Shadowfax does gig last mile and operates 4,299 physical touchpoints. Anyone who tells you these are clean categories is selling something. What is stable is not the category — it is the ratio of fixed to variable cost, and the share of the workforce that is on the books.

Follow the money

The most important thing to understand is that the ₹1,500 the consumer pays is not logistics revenue. Logistics revenue is the shipping fee — a number twenty times smaller — and it has to cover a 2,000 km journey, a doorstep visit and the possibility of doing the whole thing again in reverse.

Separate the four money flows before anything else

Flow 1 · Goods
₹1,500
Consumer → merchant. On COD, this cash is physically collected by the delivery executive and travels back through the carrier, arriving at the merchant days later.
Flow 2 · Freight
₹63
Merchant → carrier. Priced on chargeable weight (the greater of actual and volumetric), origin–destination zone, and mode.
Flow 3 · COD fee
₹20
Merchant → carrier, for cash handling and remittance risk. Typically the greater of a flat fee and a percentage of order value. Retail rate cards quote ₹25–40 or 1.5–2.5%; negotiated enterprise terms sit well below that.
Flow 4 · Failure
₹56+
Merchant → carrier, if it comes back. RTO freight is charged separately, and the merchant has also lost the sale, the packaging and the original leg.

Why this matters commercially: a merchant comparing carriers on Flow 2 alone is comparing the smallest and least variable of the four. Carrier A can be ₹20 cheaper on freight and far more expensive per order once Flow 4 is included. §11 does that arithmetic.

How this model is built, and exactly where it is guesswork

What is anchored to filings

  • Revenue per shipment. Delhivery FY26: express parcel revenue ₹6,685 Cr ÷ 1,054 mn shipments = ₹63.4. FY25: ₹5,318 Cr ÷ 752 mn = ₹70.7. Both from the Q4 & FY26 investor presentation. Filing
  • Total operating cost. Express parcel service EBITDA margin was 17.2% in FY26, so operating cost excluding corporate overhead = ₹63.4 × 82.8% = ₹52.5. Filing
  • Corporate overhead. ₹968 Cr on ₹10,439 Cr of service revenue = 9.3%, or ₹5.9 per express parcel. Filing
  • Cross-check. Ecom Express disclosed an operating cost per shipment of ₹39.65 in FY24 against revenue of ~₹50.7 — a lower-cost, lower-yield network serving deeper tier-2+ geographies. Redseer put Delhivery's FY24 express cost per shipment at ₹55.99. Two independent numbers, same order of magnitude. Filing

What is inferred

The split of that ₹52.5 across the nine stages. No Indian carrier publishes stage-level cost, and I have not found a filing that does. The allocation used here (line haul ~23%, delivery executive ~27%, sortation ~15%, first mile ~10%, destination hub ~9%, delivery centre ~7%, and the remainder across technology, support and claims) is a reasoned construction consistent with the disclosed total, with the widely-reported "last mile is roughly a third of cost" heuristic, and with the fact that Delhivery's own cost line is dominated by payments to third parties. What the merchant actually experiences

Carrier economics and merchant economics are different arithmetic on the same parcel. The merchant is the only party who loses the product margin when delivery fails, which is why merchants care about RTO roughly ten times more than carriers do.

The one sentence to remember

A carrier earns a fee once and can be made to work for it two or three times. Everything in a carrier's operating design — address intelligence, delivery slots, NDR desks, prepaid nudges, executive incentives — exists to reduce the number of times it works for the same fee.

The structural insight
Delhivery is a network orchestrator, not a fleet owner

Seventy-three paise of every rupee leaves immediately as payments to third parties. Only fifteen paise is its own payroll. It owns the sortation, the software, the customer contract and the routing decision — and rents almost everything that moves.

This is a choice with a clear logic: parcel volume is violently seasonal (festive peaks can be multiples of a normal week), and a rented network can be handed back in January. The cost of that flexibility is that most of the workforce has no attachment to the brand whose parcels they carry.

Read the payroll line again
Employee cost fell while revenue rose

₹14,368 mn → ₹13,759 mn, with revenue up 9.7%. Employee cost dropped from 17.6% to 15.4% of revenue in a single year.

Ecom Express described the same manoeuvre explicitly in its DRHP: it "reduced manpower expenses by increasing share of parcel deliveries by gig delivery partners". Two companies, same lever. Margin improvement in Indian last mile is substantially a workforce-composition story, not only an automation story. Inference

Fixed, variable, and why the distinction decides everything

Fixed — concrete and code
  • Sort centres, gateways, automated sorters
  • Warehouse and hub leases
  • Technology platform and engineering
  • Corporate overhead (₹968 Cr, 9.3% of revenue)
  • Minimum linehaul frequency on a lane

Every extra parcel through these costs almost nothing. This is where the leverage lives.

Variable — people and fuel
  • Per-parcel gig payouts
  • Contract manpower at hubs
  • Incremental linehaul trips and fuel
  • Packaging, scanning consumables
  • COD handling and remittance

Scales linearly. No leverage — only negotiation and productivity.

Semi-fixed — the dangerous middle
  • Delivery-centre rent and supervisors
  • On-roll last-mile staff
  • Minimum guarantees to partners
  • Vehicle leases and attached-fleet commitments

Steps up in chunks. A carrier that opens a delivery centre for volume that doesn't arrive carries that cost for the length of the lease.

What doubling volume does

If shipments double and fixed cost is roughly 40% of the operating base, cost per shipment falls by roughly 20% — before any negotiation or automation. That is the entire strategic case for buying market share at negative margin, and the entire reason Delhivery acquired Ecom Express rather than out-competing it. The acquired volume flows into already-built sortation.

Why this is a moat and not just an efficiency

Density compounds in a way that very few advantages do. More merchants send more parcels into the same pin codes; more parcels per pin code means shorter distance between drops; shorter distance means more drops per hour; more drops per hour means lower cost per parcel and higher earnings for the delivery executive on the same shift. Lower cost lets the carrier quote a lower rate, which wins more merchants.

The loop runs in reverse just as reliably. A sub-scale carrier in a given city has thin drops, long routes, unhappy executives who leave, worse service, and no way to price competitively without losing money on every parcel.

The corollary that carriers rarely say out loud. Because density lets the carrier reduce the per-parcel payout while total daily earnings still rise, the productivity gain is shared — but the sharing ratio is set unilaterally by the platform. Ecom Express put this in its own offer document, and it is the cleanest statement of last-mile labour economics anywhere in the Indian public record. §07 works through what it means for a worker's day.

Incentive architecture

Every incentive in last-mile delivery is an answer to a problem the company cannot solve by supervision, because the work happens alone, out of sight, hundreds of times a day.

The principal–agent problem, stated plainly

What the company wants

Every parcel delivered on the first attempt, quickly, politely, with the cash collected correctly, at the lowest possible cost per parcel, with capacity available exactly when volume spikes.

What the worker sees

A route sheet of 40 addresses, a fuel tank they paid for, an app that pays per completed delivery, four hours of daylight left, and one address on the fourth floor of a building with no lift where nobody answered last time.

The gap between those two paragraphs is the entire field of last-mile incentive design. The company cannot observe effort; it can only observe outcomes — and outcomes depend on both effort and luck. Every scheme below is an attempt to price an outcome in a way that makes the worker's best choice also the company's best choice.

The general principle

An incentive scheme is a measurement system with money attached. Whatever you can measure cheaply gets paid for, and whatever you cannot measure gets sacrificed. Indian last mile paid for throughput for a decade because parcels are trivially countable, and struggled with attempt quality because attempts were not verifiable. What changed the design space was not a change of heart — it was smartphones with GPS, cameras and call logs, which made the attempt observable for the first time.

What the mix actually looks like

So why would a carrier ever choose employees?

Choose employees when
  • The shipment is high-value or regulated — pharmaceuticals, electronics, documents
  • The customer is a business with a fixed receiving window
  • Brand promise is time-definite and a miss is expensive
  • The beat is dense and stable enough that a full shift is always full

This is Blue Dart's position, and it is why its revenue per shipment can be ₹147.

Choose gig when
  • Volume swings by multiples between a normal Tuesday and Diwali
  • Parcels are low-value and the cost of an occasional failure is small
  • Geography is being expanded faster than hiring can follow
  • Density is high enough that a partner can earn a reasonable day

This is the e-commerce parcel default, and the reason margins improved sector-wide.

Choose franchise when
  • The location is small, remote, or too thin to justify a company DC
  • Local knowledge and local hiring matter more than process control
  • You want reach without capital — DTDC's 14,000+ outlets
  • You want somebody else to absorb attendance and shrinkage risk

Cheapest route to tier-3 coverage; weakest route to consistent service.

The direction of travel, and its one counter-current. Every disclosed data point points the same way: the gig share is rising because it lowers fixed cost. But Delhivery's FY25 annual report also describes moving contractual workers into permanent roles — and the four Labour Codes now in force change the relative price of each model. The workforce mix is no longer purely a management choice. §10.

Labour economics and regulatory risk

For fifteen years the workforce model was a pure management choice. Between November 2025 and February 2026 that stopped being true. This is not legal advice — it is an attempt to size what the changes do to unit economics.

Event 1 · 21 November 2025
India's four Labour Codes came into force

The Code on Wages 2019, Code on Social Security 2020, Occupational Safety Health and Working Conditions Code 2020, and Industrial Relations Code 2020 replaced 29 central labour laws in a single commencement.

The mechanically important change is the definition of "wages": basic pay plus dearness allowance plus retaining allowance must be at least 50% of total remuneration. Indian employers have historically kept basic low — often 30–40% of CTC — to reduce provident fund, gratuity, ESI and bonus liabilities. That arbitrage is now closed.

Already visible in results. Blue Dart attributed higher employee-benefit and freight/handling costs in FY26 directly to the implementation of the new Labour Codes. Company statement

Event 2 · 13 February 2026
Karnataka's gig-worker welfare fee went live

Under the Karnataka Platform Based Gig Workers (Social Security and Welfare) Act 2025, aggregators must collect and remit a welfare fee on payouts to gig workers. The Act permits 1–5%; the notified rate is 1%, capped by category.

Independent assessment of platform working conditions

The Fairwork India Ratings 2024 — the sixth annual study by the Fairwork India team with the University of Oxford — scored 11 location-based platforms including Amazon Flex, Flipkart and Porter against five principles: fair pay, conditions, contracts, management and representation.

Findings that matter for logistics

  • Only two of eleven platforms earned the first fair-pay point for having a minimum wage policy
  • No platform earned the second fair-pay point, which requires committing to a local living wage after work-related costs
  • Ola, Uber and the logistics platform Porter scored 0 out of 10
  • The highest scores in the cohort were 6 out of 10

Why this is the right benchmark to use

Fairwork's method scores evidenced practice, not stated policy, and platforms are given the chance to submit evidence. It is the closest thing India has to an independent, repeatable audit of platform labour standards.

Its central finding — that the sector clears minimum-wage thresholds far more often than it clears living-wage-after-costs thresholds — is exactly the distinction the earnings calculator in §07 is built to make visible.

What is in "cost per delivered order"

For every 100 orders shipped, only some are delivered. The merchant pays forward freight on all 100. On the ones that return, it also pays RTO freight (modelled at 75% of forward), loses the packaging (₹18), and — critically — does not earn the gross margin on that order. Prepaid orders are modelled at a 2% RTO rate; COD at the rate you set.

Cost per delivered order = (total freight + total RTO cost + packaging on failures) ÷ orders delivered. Lost margin is shown separately because it is an opportunity cost, not a cash cost — but it is the number that actually decides which carrier is cheaper.

Illustrative throughout. The structure is what to take away: freight differences are linear in orders shipped; RTO differences are multiplied by lost margin and hit every order that fails.

The answer: a small merchant cannot buy logistics on the same terms as a large one

A carrier's sales and servicing cost is roughly fixed per account, not per parcel. Onboarding, credit assessment, API integration, a key account manager, a monthly reconciliation call — all of that costs about the same whether the merchant ships 200 parcels a month or 200,000. So carriers rationally refuse to serve small merchants directly, or serve them at retail rates.

The aggregator solves this by becoming one very large account that behaves like 215,000 small ones. It buys at enterprise rates, absorbs the servicing cost across a software platform, and resells single shipments to merchants who could never have negotiated those rates themselves. The merchant gets a better rate and better tooling than it could obtain alone. That is a real economic function, not rent-seeking.

Is it a logistics company, a software company, or a marketplace?

The case for "logistics"

Revenue is recognised gross — the full shipping charge is revenue and the courier payment is cost. That makes ₹2,024 Cr of revenue look like a logistics company's revenue, and makes the margin look thin (0.87% adjusted EBITDA).

The case for "software"

Contribution margin is 18.34% and core-business adjusted EBITDA margin is 12.56%. Revenue per employee is ₹1.48 Cr. It owns 11 warehouses through third parties and zero vehicles. Its disclosed differentiators are prediction accuracies, not pin codes.

The case for "marketplace"

Two-sided: 214,769 merchants on one side, 42 carriers on the other, with an allocation engine matching them. Concentration on both sides — top five carriers 84.5% of volume; top ten merchants 12.2% of revenue.

The defensible reading: it is a software business whose revenue is reported gross because it takes principal risk on the shipment.   • RTO risk scoring — a genuinely hard prediction over buyer history, address quality, category, price point and payment mode. Shiprocket reports 83.01% accuracy on high-risk identification.
  • Delivery-date prediction — depends on lane congestion, carrier state, day of week and season. 78.51% accuracy reported.
  • Address resolution — turning "near the big temple, opposite Sharma medical" into a rooftop coordinate. Delhivery describes a suite of fine-tuned small language models for exactly this.
Where rules are still better, and always will be
  • Serviceability — a binary fact, not a prediction. A model here would only introduce error.
  • Rate lookup — a contracted table. Nothing to learn.
  • Cut-off times and capacity — operational constraints, better expressed as hard limits than as scores.
  • Merchant policy — a business decision that should override any model, and be seen to.

Calling the whole pipeline "AI" obscures that most of it is a well-maintained rules engine — which is the harder thing to build and the more common point of failure.

Data gap 8 — allocation weights. No company discloses how it weights price against predicted success in carrier selection, and the weighting is commercially sensitive for an obvious reason: an aggregator earning a spread on each carrier has an incentive to route toward its own margin, not only toward the merchant's outcome. Whether that conflict is managed well is not verifiable from public documents. It is a good question to ask directly.

Why a merchant would choose it: speed and elasticity. If you need same-day or next-day in a metro, or you need capacity to triple for eight days in October, a gig network absorbs that in a way an employed network structurally cannot.

Why a merchant might not: service consistency. The delivering person has no exclusive relationship with the platform and may be simultaneously logged into two competitors. XpressBees — the cautionary tale

FY25 revenue ₹2,874 Cr, essentially flat against ₹2,831 Cr in FY24, with net loss widening 85% to ₹370 Cr. Courier services were 96% of operating income. Reported cause: pricing pressure in the core e-commerce parcel business as large clients renegotiated rates.

Why this matters more than the numbers. XpressBees is a well-run company with real infrastructure. Its problem is structural: in a business where cost per parcel falls with density, being the third or fourth largest network in a lane means you have the fourth-best cost structure and are still asked to match the leader's price. There is no operational excellence that fixes that. It is the clearest available demonstration that this industry has room for very few sub-scale survivors — and it is why Ecom Express was sold rather than floated.

Ekart, Amazon and the captives — now selling to everyone

Both marketplace logistics arms have opened to third parties: Ekart to MSMEs and D2C brands with 300+ franchise outlets targeting 1,000 by end-2026, and Amazon through Amazon Supply Chain Services. Ekart claims reach to more than 95% of Indian pin codes.

Why this is happening: a captive network sized for its parent's peak is idle at trough. Selling the trough capacity to outsiders is nearly pure contribution.

Why it is harder than it looks: Redseer's data puts captive pin-code reach at roughly 12,000 against 3PLs' ~27,000. Captives are dense exactly where their marketplace is dense. Growth in Indian e-commerce is coming from tier-2+, where they are thinnest. And Delhivery's management argues first-party logistics is structurally more expensive than third-party — self-serving, but consistent with the fact that a captive cannot pool demand across competing merchants and therefore cannot reach the same drop density.

India Post — the network nobody can replicate and nobody quite uses

India Post reaches every pin code in the country through the largest postal network in the world, and it is the only operator with genuine last-mile presence in the villages where e-commerce growth is now fastest. It has been used by e-commerce players for COD deliveries into deep rural geographies where no private carrier is economic.

Why merchants use it: coverage no private network matches, at a price no private network can match, in places no private network serves.

Why they mostly do not: service predictability, tracking granularity, API quality and remittance speed are not comparable to private carriers, and e-commerce merchants optimise for exactly those things.

What is actually hard to copy

A new logistics company can buy trucks, lease hubs, hire engineers and undercut on price within a year. Ranked below by what money alone cannot buy.

Difficulty of replication

Ratings are Inference — an argued ranking, not a measured one.

Genuinely hard: drop density in a specific pin code

You cannot buy it, hire it or code it. It is an emergent property of having won enough merchants whose customers happen to live near each other. It takes years, it is won street by street, and it is the only advantage that simultaneously improves the carrier's margin and the worker's earnings. Everything else on the list is downstream of it.

Genuinely hard: the address graph

Indian addresses are unstructured, informal and frequently unresolvable by any public geocoder. A carrier that has delivered to the same building 4,000 times knows things that are not in any database: which gate is open after 7pm, which flat number is mislabelled, which guard accepts parcels. Delhivery has built a suite of fine-tuned language models specifically for this. A new entrant starts with zero of it and cannot purchase it.

Easy: everything people assume is a moat

Automation is purchasable — sorters have a price list. Capital is abundant for credible teams. Tracking APIs are commodity. A pin-code list is copyable; economic service to those pin codes is not. Switching costs for merchants are genuinely low, especially through aggregators, where changing carrier is a dropdown.

The test to apply to any moat claim

Ask: "if a competitor were handed ₹2,000 crore tomorrow, how much of this could they have in eighteen months?" Sorters: all of it. Hubs: most of it. Engineers: most of it. Enterprise merchant contracts: some of it, by underpricing. Density in 18,800 pin codes and a decade of delivery outcomes attached to real addresses: none of it. That last item is the only durable answer, and it is why this industry consolidates rather than fragments.

Where AI is genuinely changing the industry

In exactly one place, and it is not routing. It is turning an unstructured Indian address into a precise location before the parcel moves. How the industry got here

Not a chronology. Each shift happened because the previous model could not solve a specific economic problem — and each one created the problem that caused the next.

PRE-2010
Document couriers meet a market that does not exist yet
Blue Dart, DTDC, First Flight and India Post move documents and B2B consignments between businesses. Recipients are offices, present during working hours, paying on invoice.
The problem this model could not solveConsumers are not offices. They are absent, they pay cash, they live at addresses that are not in any database, and they order things worth less than the cost of failing to deliver them.
2010–2014
E-commerce arrives, and COD arrives with it
Flipkart, Amazon and Snapdeal scale against a population that does not trust online payment. Cash on delivery becomes the default. Existing couriers cannot handle cash reconciliation at consumer scale, cannot serve residential addresses economically, and cannot absorb returns.
Economic problem being solvedSomebody has to build a network whose unit of work is a ₹600 parcel to a flat, collected in cash, with a one-in-four chance of coming back. Delhivery (2011), Ecom Express (2012) and Ekart are all founded in this window.
2014–2018
Marketplaces build captive networks — then discover the ceiling
Ekart and Amazon Transportation Services scale inside their parents. Control over experience is worth the capital. But a captive network can only be as dense as its own marketplace's demand in a given pin code.
The problem this createdFixed cost sized for peak, idle at trough, and structurally unable to reach the pin codes where growth would come from. Redseer would later measure the gap: ~12,000 captive pin codes against ~27,000 for 3PLs.
2015–2020
Aggregators appear, because carriers will not serve small merchants
Shiprocket and its peers discover that the constraint is not transport capacity — it is that a carrier's cost to serve an account is fixed, so 200-parcels-a-month merchants are unservable directly. The aggregator becomes one large account behaving like hundreds of thousands of small ones.
Economic problem being solvedThe mismatch between per-account servicing cost and per-parcel revenue at the small end of the market — precisely where D2C and social commerce were about to explode.
2018–2022
The gig turn
Parcel volume becomes violently seasonal as festive sales concentrate demand. Employed last-mile capacity sized for October is ruinous in February. Networks shift decisively to per-parcel gig payouts. Ecom Express's gig headcount in a single March goes from 15,479 (FY22) to 32,029 (FY24), and the gig share of its deliveries reaches 71.42%.
Economic problem being solvedConverting the largest fixed cost in the business into a variable one. It worked: sector margins improved. The cost was transferred to household budgets that now absorb demand volatility directly.
2021–2024
Automation, and the discovery that volume is the only cure
Automated sortation scales — Delhivery to 45 automated sort centres and 8.2 mn parcels/day; Ecom Express to 33 sorters and 113.77 mn parcels a month. Cost per shipment falls hard: Ecom Express from ₹47.33 (FY22) to ₹39.65 (FY24).
The problem this createdAutomation is fixed cost. It only pays if volume arrives. Companies that automated without winning volume — XpressBees, ultimately Ecom Express itself — were left with the cost structure of a giant and the volume of a challenger.
2025–2026
Consolidation, and the arrival of the regulator
Delhivery acquires Ecom Express, reported at roughly ₹1,400 Cr, and integrates it through FY26, taking Q4 express volumes up 72.5% year on year. The four Labour Codes come into force on 21 November 2025. Karnataka's gig welfare fee goes live on 13 February 2026. Shiprocket lists in August 2026; Shadowfax files. The captives open their networks to outsiders.
What is being solved nowTwo things at once: too many sub-scale networks chasing the same parcels, and a workforce model that was built entirely outside labour law. The first is being solved by acquisition. The second is being solved by legislation, slowly and unevenly.
The pattern across all seven phases. Every transition converted a fixed cost into a variable one, or pooled a fixed cost across more volume. Document couriers → parcel networks pooled the hub. Captive → 3PL pooled demand across competing merchants. Employed → gig variabilised labour. Manual → automated sortation traded variable labour for fixed capital that only pays at volume. The next transition will follow the same logic — and the largest remaining fixed-and-lumpy cost in the system is the delivery centre and the beat itself.

Where this goes next

Four forces are already visible in the disclosed numbers. Extrapolating them is not forecasting — it is reading what the filings already say.

Force 1 · Volume grows much faster than value

Shipments are projected to go from ~4.4 bn (FY24) to 15–17.5 bn by FY29 at 28–32% CAGR, while e-commerce GMV grows at ~20%. Average order value is falling from ₹1,010 toward a projected ₹680.

Consequence: more parcels, each worth less, each still requiring a person to climb a staircase. The cost per parcel must keep falling or the industry's economics invert. Every strategic move in the sector for the next five years is downstream of this single fact.

Force 2 · Growth is in the places that are hardest to serve

Tier-2+ cities went from 56% of shipments (FY21) to 62% (FY24) and are projected past 70%. These are lower-density, higher-COD, higher-RTO, longer-lane markets.

Consequence: the marginal parcel is structurally more expensive to deliver than the average one. Networks that only work at metro density will find their blended cost per shipment rising even as their metro cost falls.

Force 3 · Consolidation is not finished

One large network has been absorbed (Ecom Express), one competitor is loss-making at scale (XpressBees), and the economics reward density above all else. Redseer noted the acquisition trend as a defining feature of the sector before it happened.

Consequence: expect two or three national parcel networks, a premium specialist, several gig capacity pools, and aggregators sitting on top. Merchants should plan for a world with fewer credible alternatives and negotiate accordingly, while they still can.

Force 4 · Labour cost is becoming externally set

Labour Codes in force; a state welfare fee live and others legislating; a Social Security Fund provision on the books since 2020 permitting 1–2% of turnover; e-Shram registration at 3% of the gig workforce and rising.

Consequence: the industry's core cost lever — workforce composition — stops being purely a management decision. And because a per-worker cost floor is spread over more parcels in a dense network, regulation quietly advantages the biggest operator.

Two playbooks

The same research, read from two different chairs.

If I were launching a D2C brand tomorrow

  1. Start on an aggregator. Do not negotiate directly.Below roughly 3,000 shipments a month you will not get enterprise rates, an account manager, or priority in peak weeks from any carrier. The aggregator's margin is cheaper than the rates and tooling you would get alone.
  2. Instrument RTO from day one, by cohort.Track RTO by payment mode, by city, by product, by order value and by delivery-time band. You cannot manage this later if you did not measure it from the first hundred orders.
  3. Attack COD share before you attack shipping rates.Moving 10 points of orders from COD to prepaid is worth more than any rate negotiation you will ever win. Offer a discount smaller than your RTO cost per order and the trade pays for itself immediately.
  4. Pick two carriers and split by lane, not by loyalty.Run 70/30 from the start. You get a live benchmark, a fallback for outages, and a credible negotiating position when you are big enough to use it.
  5. Fix packaging before anything else.Weight slabs are steps, not slopes. Getting a parcel from 520g to 480g halves its rate. Volumetric weight on bulky-light goods is the single most common billing shock.
  6. Negotiate the seven things that are not the rate.RTO freight as a percentage of forward · COD remittance days · weight-dispute evidence standard · volumetric divisor · NDR reattempt SLA · liability cap · written peak capacity.
  7. Treat delivery speed as a marketing spend.Every extra day in transit adds RTO. Faster is not a luxury purchase — on the ShipNotes evidence it is one of the cheapest ways to buy conversion and reduce loss simultaneously.
  8. Reconcile every invoice, every month.Weight discrepancies, zone misclassification and surcharge creep are systematic, not random, and they are only recoverable if you catch them within the dispute window.
  9. Know your switching trigger in advance.Write down the numbers that would make you move: RTO above X%, on-time below Y%, or a rate that stops being competitive by Z%. Decide it while you are calm.
  10. Graduate deliberately.Past roughly 20,000 shipments a month, model direct contracts against your aggregator's all-in cost — including what you would have to rebuild yourself. Sometimes the answer is to stay.

If I were building a logistics company tomorrow

Do not do this
  • Do not build a national parcel network. The economics of that game are settled. You would need a decade and several thousand crore to reach a cost structure the incumbent already has, while it prices below your cost the entire way.
  • Do not compete on rate. Yields are falling by design. You would be entering a price war as the party with the worst cost position.
  • Do not assume automation is the edge. Sorters have a price list. Anyone can buy the same ones, and they only pay at volume you will not have.
Do this instead
  • Attack a cost the incumbents cannot attack without cannibalising themselves. Shared address infrastructure and pooled rural density both qualify: they are worth more to the industry than to any single participant, which is exactly why no participant will build them.
  • Sell prevention, not transport. The largest destroyable value in Indian e-commerce is the returned parcel. A business paid on RTO avoided rather than parcels moved is aligned with the merchant in a way no carrier is.
  • Own a layer, not a network. Shiprocket is worth more than several carriers that own more assets than it does. Verification, arbitration, financing and prediction are all layers with no trucks.
  • Build for tier-3 first. That is where growth is, where density is worst, and where incumbent cost structures are weakest.

  4. What proportion of your registered rider pool was active last month?

  9. Do you pay a delivery partner for a verified failed attempt? If not, what stops false NDR marking?
  10. What happens to my shipments in the second week of October when a larger customer needs capacity?

The whole industry in one sentence

Indian e-commerce logistics is a business that converts drop density into margin, funds the conversion by paying the last person in the chain per outcome rather than per hour, and loses most of what it gains to parcels that come back — which is why every serious strategic move in the sector is ultimately about density, workforce composition, or returns.

Where sources disagree

COD share of shipments

The Redseer report inside the Ecom Express DRHP states that COD accounted for over 40% of B2C e-commerce GMV in FY24 and "~27% of the total number of shipments". In the same document, Ecom Express discloses that 78.96% of its own FY24 shipments were COD.

Probable reconciliation: the two measure different populations. Redseer's denominator covers all B2C e-commerce shipments including large marketplaces with heavy prepaid mixes and their own captive logistics; Ecom Express serves a customer base skewed toward tier-2+ and value commerce, where COD dominates. Both can be right. Inference

What this means for you: COD share is a property of a merchant's customer base, not of the country. A brand selling ₹3,000 electronics to metro buyers and a brand selling ₹400 apparel into tier-3 face completely different logistics economics, and any national average will mislead both. Use your own number.

Method and limits

This document was compiled in August 2026 from public sources only. Where filings exist they are used in preference to everything else, and the specific filing and page-level context is named. Where filings do not exist — which is most of the labour economics and all of the stage-level cost data — the document says so rather than filling the gap with a plausible number.

Three limits worth stating plainly. First, the most detailed industry dataset available (Redseer) was commissioned by a company for its own offer document, and is flagged as sponsored wherever it is used. Second, the worker-economics evidence is drawn from a single large survey plus scattered reporting; it is directionally solid and specifically uncertain. Third, all illustrative models here are calibrated to disclosed totals but their internal splits are constructions — useful for understanding the mechanism, useless as market rates.
